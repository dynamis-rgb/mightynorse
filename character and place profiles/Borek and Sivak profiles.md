# Borek and Sivak

Working character and transport reference for *The Mighty Norse*.

## Established story decisions

Borek and Sivak are the two draft cattle purchased with the travelers’ vehicle and equipment in Liubice. The vehicle is intended for one animal to pull at a time. They alternate the pulling work, while the other animal walks with the party.

Their names are introduced in Chapter 4, after departure from Liubice on the journey to Rethra. Keep this placement: ordinary work, handling, and care provide a natural occasion for the reader to learn their names.

| Detail | Borek | Sivak |
|---|---|---|
| Sex | Female; a working cow | Male; an older working ox |
| Pronouns | She / her | He / him |
| Age | Mature, normal working age; exact age undecided | Old, with years of work behind him; exact age undecided |
| Work | Pulls the vehicle in alternation with Sivak | Pulls the vehicle in alternation with Borek, with effort adjusted to his condition |

Retain the names Borek and Sivak as supplied in the manuscript. Their linguistic origins and historical forms have not been verified.

## Borek

Borek is the younger animal, but she is an experienced working adult rather than an untrained youngster. Her female sex is an intentional story choice. Cows can perform draft work; she does not need to become male to make the transport arrangement plausible.

She can take the longer or more demanding turns when Sivak needs relief, provided the load remains within her capacity. Do not treat her as inexhaustible simply because she is younger.

Her reproductive and milking status are not established. Do not assume she supplies milk merely because she is female; any lactation would need its own explanation and continuity.

The manuscript gives her a low, dissatisfied sound during a climb and a response to Friðríkr’s attention. Develop her presence through such observable behavior without making every sound a humanlike answer.

## Sivak

Sivak is the older male. Use him as a castrated working ox rather than an intact breeding bull. His exact age remains open; age, condition, and accumulated wear should be conveyed through his behavior rather than a precise number unless the story needs one.

He remains useful when purchased. His experience can coexist with reduced endurance: familiar commands and a settled working routine do not imply that he can sustain Borek’s workload.

Possible signs of declining capacity include a slower start after rest, a need for shorter pulling turns, or longer recovery after difficult ground. These are options for future characterization, not symptoms already established in the prose. If the planned later death is assigned to Sivak, prepare it through a coherent decline rather than age alone.

## Vehicle and working arrangement

The intended arrangement is two animals available for one pulling position. They are not routinely yoked side by side.

- A light two-wheeled cart with shafts and a single-animal yoke is the recommended physical design. Confirm the number of wheels against existing descriptions before making this a final vehicle specification.
- Switch animals at substantial rests, according to terrain, fatigue, and condition. Equal turns need not be equal in length or effort.
- The animal out of harness still walks the route. Alternation provides relief from pulling, not a complete rest from travel.
- Someone leads or otherwise controls the spare animal, especially at crossings, on narrow tracks, and among other traffic.
- The yoke must fit the animal wearing it. Separate fitted single yokes are a useful option if their sizes differ appreciably; the exact equipment inventory remains to be settled.
- Keep the load modest. The companions generally walk and can push or carry goods over difficult stretches.
- Allow time for water, grazing, feeding, hoof inspection, and recovery. Neither animal should function as transport that needs attention only when something goes wrong.

## Terminology

For precise collective narration, use **the cattle**, **the draft animals**, **the pair**, or their names. Borek is a cow; Sivak is an ox. This avoids implying that both are castrated males when the narrative calls them “the oxen.” Characters may use less precise everyday language where appropriate.

Use **cart** consistently if the two-wheeled design is adopted. Existing references to a wagon should be checked against the chosen construction rather than replaced blindly.

## Manuscript continuity notes

These are identified corrections for a later manuscript pass; this profile does not change the prose.

1. Check the Liubice purchase and later harnessing scenes for a single-animal vehicle and yoke, rather than a double yoke or an established side-by-side team.
2. When the later outline’s animal death and replacement purchase are developed, identify which animal dies, what happens to the survivor, and which animal pulls each vehicle if the group acquires a second one.

## Historical basis and limits

Single-animal cattle harnesses and draft work by cows are practically sound. European single-animal traction between shafts predates the story, although the historical study below chiefly concerns equids. The exact combination of a cow, an older ox, alternating shifts, and a single-animal cart in Liubice in 867 is a plausible story reconstruction rather than a directly documented local practice.
